## The format, and why it matters to us

He restarts at beginner rating and climbs a band at a time under a **published
rule set he is not allowed to break**, then loosens the rules as the rating rises.
The rules are the product; the games are the demonstration. Explicitly stated in
the intro: the goal isn't to win every game, it's to teach high-percentage moves
that are right most of the time.

That is a rating-banded curriculum with a stated contract per band — structurally
much closer to what this app's ladder wants than a speedrun narrated ad hoc. It is
the most directly reusable thing on the channel.

## Level 1 — the opening contract (roughly ≤1000)

- Knights before bishops, every time.
- You always need a pawn in the centre; if you don't have one, getting one is the
  move.
- Never develop a piece to a square in front of your own centre pawn — it blocks
  the pawn you just fought for.
- Castle early; he treats it as non-negotiable rather than situational.
- After development, rooks go to the middle — the open or soon-to-open files.
- Don't move the same piece twice in the opening without a concrete reason.

## Level 2-3 — trades, tempo, and the first tactics

- Trade when you're ahead in material; avoid trades when you're behind. Stated as
  a hard default, not a preference.
- A rook coming to the centre **with check** is a free tempo — if the enemy king
  is still uncastled, do it before they castle, because the same move is worth
  much less afterwards.
- Getting a queen trade sometimes costs a knight move first; the knight move is
  worth it.
- Recapture toward the centre as a default.
- Watch the pin on a developed knight: if the opponent piles on it faster than you
  can unpin, the pin wins material regardless of the count.

## Level 4 — the concepts he names explicitly (roughly 1600+)

Named in the level-four intro:
- Knight versus bishop — which minor piece the structure favours.
- How to play against weak pawns.
- How to exploit weak squares.
- Don't trade when down material.
- Master the checkmating patterns cold.

## Level 5+ — endgame confidence as a strategic asset

A recurring, and unusually honest, argument: under roughly 2000 nobody knows what
to do in an endgame, so a player with drilled endgame habits should *seek*
simplification even when the material edge is small, because the technique gap is
worth more than the extra piece the opponent is clinging to. He frames trading
into an endgame as the reward for having done the work, not as a concession.

## Register notes (how he talks)

- Rule-first, then the demonstration. He states the habit, plays the move, and
  names which habit it was — a natural fit for a two-register beat (`say` carries
  the habit, `sayShort` names it in a few words).
- Heavy use of "always" and "never" as teaching absolutes, then explicit
  loosening at the next band ("level one would never feature this move"). The
  contradiction is deliberate and pedagogical: the habit is a scaffold to be
  outgrown, and he says so.
- He narrates his own mistakes without softening them, including losses to lower
  rated opponents, and extracts the habit that failed.
- Chat interjections, sub shout-outs and music breaks are woven through
  everything — roughly a third to a half of the word count on the speedrun-style
  series is not chess. Mining is mandatory; reading raw transcripts is a waste.
