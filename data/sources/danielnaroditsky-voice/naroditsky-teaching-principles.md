## Source 1 — Listudy: "25 Lessons from Daniel Naroditsky's Speedrun"
URL: https://listudy.org/en/blog/daniel-naroditsky-speedrun-principles

Recurring phrasings + the concepts behind them:

1. **Piece-hierarchy in defense.** "The least valuable piece should perform
   the task." → defensive duties go to lower-value pieces so the more
   valuable ones stay flexible.

2. **You don't always have to react.** Continue executing your plan unless
   the threat is immediate; reactive moves often surrender the initiative.

3. **A "hook" is a pawn next to your opponent's king.** Advanced enemy
   pawns are levers/targets — attack lever for opening the king position.

4. **Successful attacks need TWO things: pieces and weaknesses.** Counting
   only attackers (without targeting real weaknesses) is wishful thinking;
   counting weaknesses without enough pieces in range is impotent.

5. **Kinetic vs potential energy.** Pieces blocking their own pawns LOOK
   passive; they're storing energy that unleashes when the position opens.

6. **Endgame: promote the pawn.** The whole point of an endgame is making
   a passed pawn a queen — every piece evaluation is "does this help that?"

7. **Use your passive pieces.** When a task needs doing, send the worst-
   placed piece — your best pieces are doing the work that matters; keep
   them on their squares.

8. **Carve and undermine.** Pawn moves that sacrifice structure to GAIN a
   better outpost or unhinge the opponent's pawn chain are routine, not
   exotic.

9. **Plans longer than ~3 moves usually aren't.** Realistic chess plans
   are short with high adaptability; long visions are fantasy.

10. **Symptom vs cause of a tactic.** You can stop the move (block the
    diagonal) OR remove the reason it works (trade off the attacker).
    Removing the cause is sturdier.

11. **Space and time = money.** Positional advantages must convert to
    something concrete (material, mate, structural ruin) or they evaporate.

12. **Static vs dynamic factors.** Dynamic = expires; static = permanent.
    Cash in dynamic edges (initiative, lead in development) before they
    drain; build static ones (structure, weak squares) to keep forever.

13. **The wishlist method.** Don't calculate forward — start from the
    position you WANT, then work backwards to find moves that get there.

14. **Be flexible.** A plan you've committed to that isn't working is a
    plan you're losing with. Switch.

15. **Flank attack → center counter.** When your opponent commits pieces to
    the wing, the center is undefended. Strike there.

16. **Pin assessment.** A pin against a pawn-defended piece is much less
    dangerous than against an undefended one — the defender is the brake.

17. **Inaccessibility.** A weak square the opponent can't actually
    reach (no piece can get there in time, no pawn break opens the diagonal)
    is not a real weakness — it's a theoretical one.

18. **"What if we do it anyway?"** When a move LOOKS forbidden (a tactic
    prevents it), check whether the tactic actually works. Frequently
    the prevention is illusory.

## Voice register notes

- **Direct, second-person to the student** ("you", "your pieces", "we") —
  he is COACHING, not lecturing.
- Sentences are usually SHORT and DECLARATIVE. Long sentences when he
  walks through a concrete sequence; short ones when he names a principle.
- Catch-phrases recur: "the point is", "here's the key", "what we want",
  "listen", "the idea is".
- Uses both **chess-specific terms** ("the f6-knight is overloaded") and
  **plain-English metaphors** ("the pawn is a wedge", "the king is
  exposed and his pieces aren't talking to each other").
- Avoids generic praise ("Great move!"); names WHAT the move accomplished
  ("Now Black's queenside is buried").
- When teaching variations, explicit on the FORK: "If they go for X, we
  do Y. If they prefer Z, we have W."
